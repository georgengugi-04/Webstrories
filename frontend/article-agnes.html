What Agnes Kariuki Wants to Know Isn't on the Label

Agnes Kariuki runs a grocery shop, sourcing fresh produce the way a small supermarket does. Her question wasn't about price or supply. It was about what had actually been sprayed on the produce before it reached her shelves.

Interviewer George Ngugi / Field Research, GreenTrack / Kenya
Field note. No photograph was taken during this conversation with Agnes. Everything below reflects what she shared, written up honestly rather than illustrated with a photo that doesn't exist.
George Ngugi
Interviewer / Field Researcher, GreenTrack
This story is based on a conversation with Agnes Kariuki, who runs a grocery shop, as part of GreenTrack's ongoing research with the people who sell produce, not just the people who grow it.

Agnes Kariuki's shop isn't a roadside stall. It runs closer to how a small supermarket sources fresh produce — buying in on a more structured, ongoing basis, with an expectation of consistency her customers have come to rely on.

That structure is exactly what shaped her question. Where other conversations on this research trip circled around supply and timing, Agnes's concern was different: she wanted to know what chemicals had actually been used on the produce she was stocking. Not whether it looked fresh, not whether it was priced right — whether it was safe, and whether anyone besides the grower actually knew.

Field Insight — a synthesized observation from this visit, not a direct quote

A shop owner can vouch for freshness by looking. She can't vouch for what was sprayed weeks earlier by looking at all.

It's a question that sits upstream of almost everything else GreenTrack has heard in this research. Erah's plot, Jonathan's coffee, John's maize and ginger and bananas — all of it eventually passes through someone like Agnes, someone who has to stand behind produce she didn't grow and can't independently verify. Right now, that trust runs on reputation and habit, not on any record she can actually check.

This is a different shape of the same underlying gap GreenTrack keeps running into: growers keep records for themselves, on their own plots, for their own purposes. Agnes's question is a reminder that those same records — what was applied, and when — matter just as much to the person selling the harvest as they do to the person who grew it.

What this conversation surfaced
  • Retailers need visibility into chemical and pesticide use, not just growers and regulators
  • Trust in produce safety currently runs on reputation, not on any checkable record
  • A grower's treatment log has value well beyond the farm gate — a shop owner is a real audience for it too
